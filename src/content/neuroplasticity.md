# The Inner World and the Outer Life
### Neuroplasticity, Hermetic philosophy, and the practice of conscious transformation

Before a vision becomes something we build, it exists as something we imagine.

A business begins as an idea. A skill begins with an intention to learn. A different way of living often begins when we question a familiar belief about who we are and what is possible.

Spiritual philosophies invite us to examine this creative relationship between consciousness and experience. Neuroscience contributes a complementary discovery: experience can change the brain itself.

At Mentis Forge, we explore the conversation between these perspectives. Spirituality offers questions of meaning, intention, and consciousness. Science provides methods for investigating particular mechanisms of perception, learning, and change.

## The principle of Mentalism

*The Kybalion*, published in 1908, expresses its principle of Mentalism through the statement: “The All is Mind; the Universe is Mental.” Within its philosophy, mind is fundamental to the nature of reality. This is a metaphysical proposition about existence, extending far beyond the claim that our personal attitudes influence our lives.

Mel Gill’s *The Meta Secret* draws on a framework of seven Hermetic principles, encouraging readers to understand personal transformation through ideas including Mentalism, Correspondence, Vibration, Rhythm, Polarity, and Cause and Effect. These teachings provide the spiritual lens for this exploration. Their presentation as universal laws belongs to that philosophical framework; it does not give them the same experimental standing as laws in physics.

A practical question emerges from Mentalism:

**What kind of life are we helping to cultivate through the thoughts we entertain, the meanings we assign, and the actions we repeatedly take?**

We can explore that question without first resolving whether consciousness is the foundation of the universe.

## Neuroplasticity: experience leaves a biological imprint

Neuroplasticity is the nervous system’s capacity to change in response to experience. Research has demonstrated changes in synaptic communication and measurable adaptations associated with learning.

In one influential study, adults learning to juggle developed selective, transient changes in brain regions associated with processing visual motion. In another, adults who successfully learned London’s demanding taxi-navigation requirements showed changes in the posterior hippocampus over several years of training. These findings demonstrate that the adult brain remains responsive to what we learn and practise.

The spiritual significance we might draw from this is an interpretation: **transformation can involve becoming an active participant in our own development.**

The scientific finding is more specific: learning and experience can alter aspects of brain function and structure. It does not establish that intention alone reorganizes external events.

## Internal projections and experienced reality

The world we experience includes both what happens and how we interpret what happens.

Research on perception shows that expectations can prepare sensory systems for anticipated information. In controlled experiments, researchers have detected brain activity representing expected visual features before the stimulus appears. Our experience therefore involves an interaction between incoming information and internal expectations.

Consider two people encountering the same setback.

One interprets it as evidence that they are incapable. The other interprets it as information about what they need to learn. Their interpretations may lead to different actions: withdrawal, experimentation, seeking guidance, or trying again.

This example illustrates a practical route through which an inner outlook can influence an outer life. It is a synthesis of psychological ideas, rather than a guarantee that a particular mindset produces a particular outcome.

From a spiritual perspective, we might describe this as bringing awareness to our internal projections. In practical terms, it means asking:

*What am I assuming? What else could this mean? What response would serve the life I want to build?*

## Correspondence: examining what we bring into our relationships

The principle of Correspondence invites reflection on relationships between different levels of experience. Here, we can use it as a contemplative lens for examining connections between our inner patterns and outward conduct.

For example, a person who expects criticism may approach a conversation defensively. That defensiveness can affect how the other person responds. Recognizing the expectation creates an opportunity to enter the next conversation differently.

This is an illustrative feedback loop. It does not mean every difficult relationship is a reflection of an internal flaw, or that external circumstances are entirely self-created.

A useful spiritual practice is to examine our contribution while remaining attentive to the independent choices of others.

## Vibration: spiritual language and measurable rhythms

Within spiritual language, “vibration” often describes the felt quality of a person’s presence: peaceful, agitated, compassionate, fearful, or purposeful.

Within neuroscience, frequency has a precise meaning. Neural activity can exhibit measurable oscillations, and experiments have investigated how their timing relates to perception and attention. Research on alpha oscillations, for example, has connected experimentally induced rhythmic activity with fluctuations in visual awareness.

These findings do not demonstrate that love, fear, or intention broadcasts a frequency that attracts matching circumstances.

For this article, **“raising our vibration” can be used as an explicitly spiritual metaphor for cultivating the qualities we wish to embody**. Its practical expression might be listening more carefully, responding with greater patience, or following through on a meaningful commitment.

The metaphor can carry personal meaning while the scientific terminology retains its precision.

## Visualization: giving intention a form we can practise

Visualization offers a compelling meeting point between inner experience and observable learning.

Research comparing mental and physical practice of a finger exercise found that mental rehearsal could alter measures of the motor system involved in the task. This supports a role for imagined practice in specific forms of learning.

A spiritual practitioner might understand visualization as aligning with a future possibility. A scientific account investigates the cognitive and neural processes involved in rehearsing an action. These interpretations can coexist, provided we do not treat one experiment as proof of the entire spiritual explanation.

A grounded practice is to imagine the process in detail: preparing, acting, encountering difficulty, adjusting, and continuing.

Olympic swimmer Michael Phelps has described rehearsing different race scenarios mentally alongside his extensive physical preparation. His experience offers an example of mental rehearsal within accomplished performance. It cannot establish how much visualization independently caused his success.

## Conscious creation as a practice

For Mentis Forge, conscious creation can mean deliberately participating in the development of our lives.

We clarify what matters. We examine the beliefs guiding our decisions. We rehearse useful responses, practise necessary skills, and revise our approach when experience teaches us something new.

Here, intention supplies direction. Action gives that direction expression. Feedback helps us remain connected to reality.

This understanding also leaves room for compassion. Illness, hardship, injustice, and loss cannot responsibly be reduced to someone’s thoughts or “frequency.” Personal agency exists alongside circumstances we did not choose.

## An invitation to explore

Neuroplasticity establishes that experience can change the brain. Research on perception shows that expectations participate in how we process the world. Spiritual teachings ask us to consider the deeper significance of consciousness, intention, and our relationship with existence.

The connections between these perspectives invite exploration; they do not make them interchangeable.

Our guiding proposition is that **cultivating the inner world can change how we participate in the outer one**. Whether someone understands that process through spiritual alignment, psychological learning, or both, it becomes meaningful through lived practice: greater awareness, deliberate action, and a willingness to grow.
