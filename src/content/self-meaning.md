# Self & Meaning
### The neuroscience and psychology of identity, values, and meaning

Who are you beneath the roles you occupy, the expectations you carry, and the stories you have learned to tell about yourself?

The question can arise quietly during reflection or urgently when life changes. A relationship ends. A familiar ambition loses its appeal. An experience reveals a possibility we had never considered.

Through a spiritual lens, these moments invite self-discovery: an examination of what feels deeply meaningful and how we wish to live. Psychology explores how identity develops through experience, relationships, and personal narratives. Neuroscience investigates the processes involved when we remember ourselves, evaluate what matters, and imagine our future.

Together, these perspectives offer a way to explore **who we understand ourselves to be—and who we choose to become.**

## Identity: the story we live within

Our sense of identity includes memories, relationships, commitments, cultural influences, and beliefs about our qualities and abilities.

Some descriptions feel simple: *I am a parent. I am a builder. I am someone who values independence.* Others carry assumptions that deserve examination: *I always fail. I must please everyone. People like me cannot change.*

Psychologists Dan McAdams and Kate McLean describe **narrative identity** as an evolving life story through which people connect their reconstructed past with an imagined future. This story helps provide continuity and purpose across changing circumstances. It is shaped within a social and cultural world, rather than authored in isolation.

Spiritually, we might approach this story as something worthy of compassionate inquiry.

Which parts express our considered values? Which reflect expectations we inherited? Which once helped us navigate a difficult situation but now deserve reconsideration?

**Self-discovery can begin with listening carefully to the explanations we give for who we are.**

## The brain reflecting on itself

When we think about ourselves, the brain engages processes that researchers can investigate experimentally.

In a study asking participants to judge whether personality traits described them, researchers found engagement of the medial prefrontal cortex during self-referential processing. Other research has implicated a broader network in different forms of thinking about the self.

These findings help explain aspects of self-reflection. They do not locate a complete identity—or a soul—in a single brain region.

The spiritual question of what the self ultimately is remains broader than these experiments can answer. What science can examine is how particular experiences of selfhood relate to memory, evaluation, and other measurable processes.

This leaves room for a thoughtful relationship between inquiry and mystery: studying the mechanisms of experience while remaining open about its deeper significance.

## Values: discovering what deserves our commitment

Identity concerns who we understand ourselves to be. Values concern what we consider worth caring about.

Honesty, compassion, freedom, learning, creativity, and service can each provide direction. Their meaning becomes clearer when circumstances ask something of us.

Honesty may require acknowledging a mistake. Compassion may ask us to listen when we would prefer to judge. Learning may require admitting that our understanding is incomplete.

Researchers studying **values-based self-affirmation** have asked participants to reflect on personally important values. One brain-imaging study found greater activity in regions associated with self-related processing and valuation when affirmed participants considered future situations involving their core values. This is a specific experimental finding, rather than evidence that repeating any positive statement produces transformation.

Through a spiritual lens, values can serve as a compass: a way to orient ourselves when approval, convenience, and conviction pull in different directions.

Useful questions include:

- What do I want my conduct to express?
- What matters to me when recognition is absent?
- Which commitments am I willing to make time for?
- Where could my actions better reflect what I say I value?

**A value becomes part of our lived identity when we give it expression.**

## Meaning: understanding, direction, and significance

Meaning is often discussed as though it were one feeling or one discovery. Psychologists Frank Martela and Michael Steger distinguish three related dimensions: **coherence, purpose, and significance**.

### Coherence: can I make sense of my life?

Coherence concerns whether life feels understandable.

We may seek it by reflecting on our experiences, recognizing influences, and connecting events into a larger account. Understanding can remain partial. We can acknowledge uncertainty without forcing every experience into a reassuring explanation.

Spiritually, this may involve learning to hold questions patiently while continuing to live with care.

### Purpose: what gives my life direction?

Purpose concerns the aims and commitments that organize our efforts.

It may take shape through raising a family, practising a craft, studying, building something useful, or contributing to a community. A purpose can develop through participation: we encounter a responsibility or possibility, invest ourselves in it, and discover that it matters.

### Significance: does my life matter?

Significance concerns the sense that one’s life has value and importance.

Within a spiritual understanding, this may be grounded in sacredness, belonging, or connection to something greater than oneself. Those are philosophical or faith-based interpretations.

In everyday life, we can also reflect on the relationships and responsibilities through which we experience mattering: someone trusts us, our presence brings comfort, or our contribution helps sustain something we care about.

These three dimensions can offer different questions when meaning feels distant: *Do I need understanding, direction, or a stronger sense of connection and worth?*

## The observing self: creating room for reflection

A contemplative approach invites us to notice our thoughts, emotions, and self-descriptions as experiences we can examine.

Instead of immediately accepting *I am a failure* as a complete account of ourselves, we might notice: *I am having a painful thought about what happened.*

That small change in wording creates an invitation to investigate. What happened? What responsibility belongs to us? What can we learn? What remains possible?

As a spiritual practice, observing our inner life can help us meet ourselves with greater patience. It need not require rejecting personality or ambition. We can remain committed to our lives while becoming less rigid about the stories through which we interpret them.

## Growth: revising the story honestly

Reconsidering identity asks for both imagination and honesty.

A person who has repeatedly avoided difficult conversations might begin practising direct, respectful communication. Their previous behaviour remains part of their history. New actions can provide material for a more complete understanding of themselves.

The emerging story might become: *I have struggled with this, and I am learning to respond differently.*

This is a practical illustration of revising a self-narrative. It does not promise that adopting a new label will automatically change behaviour.

A useful reflection holds three things together:

**What has shaped me? What matters to me now? What will I practise next?**

The past supplies context. Values offer direction. Present action gives development a concrete form.

## Meaning in difficult experiences

Pain can disrupt our understanding of ourselves and our place in the world. It may unsettle beliefs that once felt secure.

A spiritual exploration can make room for grief, anger, uncertainty, and questions without requiring an immediate lesson. Nobody needs to describe suffering as a gift or assume it was deserved.

Over time, a person may discover a commitment that grows from their experience: offering support they once needed, changing a harmful pattern, or protecting something they now understand more deeply.

Meaning can be something we gradually build in response to what happened. Its timing and form remain personal.

## A practice of self and meaning

Set aside a quiet moment and consider four reflections.

**The story.**  
What description of myself do I repeat most often? What experiences support it, and what experiences complicate it?

**The values.**  
Choose three qualities you want your life to express. Describe what each would look like in an ordinary day.

**The direction.**  
What relationship, responsibility, question, or project deserves more of your attention?

**The action.**  
Choose one manageable step: keep a promise, ask an honest question, make time to study, or offer practical help.

Return to these reflections as your circumstances change. Allow your understanding to deepen through what you actually experience.

## Living an examined life

Self and meaning meet in the relationship between our understanding and our conduct.

We remember, interpret, imagine, and choose. We inherit stories and develop our own. We discover commitments through relationships, reflection, and participation.

The spiritual invitation is to bring awareness to that process—to listen inwardly while remaining responsive to the people and world around us.

**Who we have been gives us a history. What we value gives us direction. How we live gives those values expression.**
